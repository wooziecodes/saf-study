## Executive Summary

## Working Hypothesis

The Americas are likely to be one of the most important SAF supply regions through 2030, but the opportunity for Singapore is shifting. The previous route — Asia-origin used cooking oil (UCO) aggregated through Singapore and sold into the U.S. renewable fuels market — is becoming less attractive as U.S. policy favours North American feedstock. Singapore should therefore focus less on pass-through UCO trade into the U.S., and more on building a role in new SAF corridors linking Latin American feedstock, production projects and offtake demand in Europe and Asia.

Two implications follow directly from the U.S. feedstock re-nationalisation described in Section 2.2:

1. **Singapore's trading and blending value proposition needs to re-orient toward Europe- and Asia-Pacific-bound cargoes** rather than U.S.-bound ones, since non-North American UCO no longer qualifies for the 45Z credit.
2. **The same North American-content logic that excludes Asian UCO from U.S. tax credits creates an opening for Latin American feedstock** (Brazilian soybean oil and macaúba, Argentine soy oil already flowing into Canada) to fill the North American gap — a trade Singapore-based commodity houses are well placed to help finance and structure, even without touching Singapore soil. Canada's Braya Renewable Fuels facility is the clearest live example: it sources soy oil imported from Argentina (757,000 tonnes in 2024, up from 48,000 tonnes in 2023) because domestic Canadian feedstock cannot support its capacity, and exports 100% of its output to the U.S.

Brazil should be the priority market for Singapore over 2026–2030, with the U.S. remaining the largest existing production base (now reoriented around North American feedstock) and Canada a smaller, credit-market-driven opportunity tied closely to U.S. dynamics. Colombia and Chile are monitored for longer-term opportunities (Section 5); Mexico remains a policy-trigger watch item with no near-term production case.

Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) is a substitute for conventional jet fuel made from waste oils, biomass or synthetic pathways. It can reduce lifecycle emissions by roughly 60–80% compared with fossil kerosene, depending on feedstock and production pathway. SAF has become a core policy tool for aviation decarbonisation because airlines have few near-term alternatives for cutting long-haul emissions. In the Americas, the United States has the largest existing production base, Brazil has the clearest feedstock and policy advantages, and Canada is developing a more targeted position in the value chain, currently constrained by a credit market where SAF is a negligible share of activity. The main negative shift for Singapore is in the U.S., where new rules reduce the attractiveness of non-North American feedstock, including Asian UCO flows that Singapore-based trading houses have previously helped move.

This report examines the United States, Brazil and Canada in depth, and assesses where Singapore-based companies can participate credibly over 2026–2030.

Three judgments frame the analysis:

1. **U.S. SAF growth will increasingly rely on North American feedstock.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) restricts the 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit to feedstock grown or produced in the U.S., Canada or Mexico from 2026, and cuts the maximum SAF credit from $1.75 to $1.00 per gallon.
2. **Brazil is the best-positioned Americas market.** Acelen/Mubadala's planned $3 billion+, 1-billion-litre-per-year Bahia biorefinery, together with Brazil's legislated SAF emissions-reduction pathway to 2037, gives Brazil the strongest mix of feedstock availability, policy certainty and project scale.
3. **Canada's SAF volume today is negligible and its near-term outlook is bifurcated.** British Columbia will generate mandated SAF demand from 2028; the rest of the country relies on a federal credit market (Clean Fuel Regulations) in which SAF contributed just 0.8% of credit-generating fuel in 2024. Canada's relevance to Singapore is currently more about the Argentina–Canada feedstock corridor than about Canadian SAF production itself.

## 1. Background & Key Objectives

We are assessing the Sustainable Aviation Fuel opportunity across the Americas to determine where Singapore-based companies (trading houses, feedstock aggregators, technology licensors, investors and the aviation/bunkering ecosystem) can most credibly participate in the region's build-out. This draft narrows the deep-dive scope to the three markets carrying the most near-term volume and Singapore relevance — the U.S., Brazil and Canada — while keeping Chile, Colombia and Mexico as a shorter watchlist (Section 5) so that work already done on those markets isn't lost.

### 1.1 What Biofuels Are, and Why They Are Growing

Biofuels are fuels derived from biomass or organic waste rather than fossil sources. They include ethanol from sugarcane or corn, biodiesel and renewable diesel from vegetable oils, waste oils and animal fats, biomethane, and Sustainable Aviation Fuel. Demand across the Americas is being driven by three main factors:

- **Policy mandates and tax credits.** Blending mandates, such as Brazil's E30/B15 requirements and Canada's Clean Fuel Regulations, and production tax credits, such as the U.S. 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit, are turning biofuels from a voluntary SAF sustainability purchase into a compliance-driven market with more predictable demand.
- **Energy security.** Governments are also using biofuels to reduce exposure to imported crude and oil-price volatility. Brazil's ethanol and biodiesel programme is the clearest example, and similar concerns are now shaping U.S. and Canadian policy.
- **Decarbonisation commitments.** ICAO's CORSIA scheme becomes mandatory for all member states from 2027, after the voluntary phase from 2021–2026. IATA members have also committed to net-zero aviation emissions by 2050, with SAF expected to deliver roughly 65% of the required emissions reduction.

### 1.2 Introduction to SAF

SAF is a "drop-in" fuel: it is chemically compatible with existing jet engines and airport fuelling infrastructure at blend ratios of up to 50%, and can substitute for part of the fossil content in conventional jet fuel. It matters because aviation has limited near-term alternatives to liquid fuels, especially for long-haul flights. Unlike road transport, commercial aviation cannot rely on battery electrification at scale in the near term.

**HEFA vs. Power-to-Liquid: the two pathways that matter.** Nearly all SAF produced today, and essentially all of the volume discussed in this report's country sections, is made via one of two pathway families:

| Dimension | HEFA (Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids) | Power-to-Liquid (PtL / e-SAF) |
|---|---|---|
| Feedstock | Used cooking oil, animal tallow, vegetable oils | Green hydrogen (via electrolysis) + captured CO₂ |
| Technology maturity | Commercial-scale today; >80% of global SAF supply | Pilot/early-commercial; first integrated plant (Chile) reached FID in 2021 |
| Relative cost vs. fossil jet fuel | ~2–5x (2024 EASA reference: €2,085/t vs €734/t fossil) | Up to ~12x (2024 EASA reference: €7,695/t) |
| Scalability constraint | Finite global waste-oil pool; competes with renewable diesel for the same feedstock | Capital intensity and green hydrogen/renewable power availability |
| Americas relevance | Dominant pathway in the U.S., Brazil, Canada | Early-stage; Chile (Haru Oni) is the regional proof point (Section 5) |

*Sources: International Council on Clean Transportation, "Why and how to bring down the cost of SAF," theicct.org, Nov 2025; S&P Global, "Volatile sustainable aviation fuel prices highlight scaling challenges," Jan 2026; Carbon Direct, "The SAF cost premium is permanent," June 2026.*

For Brazil in particular, a third pathway — **Alcohol-to-Jet (ATJ)**, which converts sugarcane or corn ethanol into jet fuel — is strategically important given the country's existing ethanol infrastructure, and is discussed in the Brazil deep dive.

**The SAF value chain** runs from (i) feedstock collection — restaurant/food-service used cooking oil, rendering-plant tallow, oilseed crushing, or green hydrogen/DAC for e-fuels; (ii) pre-treatment and refining at a HEFA, ATJ or Fischer-Tropsch unit (often a converted or co-located renewable diesel plant); (iii) blending with conventional Jet A-1 at certified ratios; (iv) certification and environmental-attribute tracking (RSB, ISCC, CORSIA-eligibility, book-and-claim SAF certificates); and (v) offtake by airlines or, increasingly, corporate Scope 3 buyers via book-and-claim arrangements — as in Google's June 2026 purchase of SAF certificates linked to 35 million gallons of American Airlines SAF, transacted through the **SAFc Registry** (docs.safcregistry.org), a book-and-claim registry founded by RMI and the Environmental Defense Fund. The original draft referenced this deal without naming the registry behind it; it is worth citing directly given Singapore's own book-and-claim ambitions (Section 3, U.S. Singapore Opportunity).

## 2. SAF Market Context

### 2.1 Unit Economics and Pricing

SAF is likely to remain more expensive than fossil jet fuel because feedstocks are limited, production pathways are capital intensive, and demand is still largely policy-driven rather than purely commercial.

- EASA's 2024 reference price for HEFA-based SAF was €2,085/tonne against €734/tonne for conventional jet fuel — roughly a 3x premium — while e-SAF/PtL was priced at €7,695/tonne, roughly 10x.
- IATA estimates airlines paid a collective USD 2.9 billion premium for the 1.9 million tonnes of SAF available in 2025, of which USD 1.4 billion was the structural price spread over fossil jet.
- Global SAF production is forecast at only 2.4 million tonnes in 2026 — about 0.8% of total jet fuel consumption — with IATA warning that growth is slowing just as e-SAF sub-mandates approach in the UK (2028) and EU (2030).
- Platts' SAF price assessment (CIF Northwest Europe) has shown roughly 4.7x the volatility of fossil jet fuel since its September 2023 launch (a $444/mt standard deviation versus $95/mt for fossil jet) — a function of a thin, early-stage, oligopolistic market. **Note:** Platts moved to a new CIF NW Europe methodology in March 2025 (priced as a premium to the Jet CIF NWE forward curve); the live series should be pulled directly rather than relying on this single cited statistic in the next pass.
- Argus Media publishes a US-specific benchmark (Argus Americas Biofuels) that fills the gap left by Platts' Europe-only coverage — worth adding to the pricing picture once budget/access is confirmed.

Two policy models have emerged for managing this premium: Europe's "polluter pays" approach (ReFuelEU Aviation mandates place the cost on fuel suppliers, who pass it to airlines and passengers) versus the U.S. approach of taxpayer-funded production credits (45Z, formerly 40B) that socialise the premium. Singapore has adopted a third, hybrid model: a fixed, centrally-procured passenger/cargo levy (Section 2.4).

### 2.2 Feedstock and SAF Trade Flows

For Singapore, the key trade-flow issue in 2025–2026 is the overlap between rising global demand for used cooking oil (UCO), U.S.-China tariff escalation, and the new U.S. domestic-content requirement under the 45Z tax credit.

**The U.S. feedstock re-nationalisation.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, restricts 45Z-eligible feedstock to material "produced or grown" in the United States, Canada or Mexico from 2026 onward. This excludes sugarcane, soybeans, tallow and used oils sourced from outside North America.

This is already changing trade flows, though the picture requires two separate statistics to be read together rather than in isolation:

- **U.S.-bound Chinese UCO exports fell 55% year-on-year**, from 1.2 million tonnes in Jan–Nov 2024 to 540,000 tonnes over the same period in 2025, as tariffs and the coming 45Z restriction pushed volumes elsewhere.
- **China's *global* UCO exports were up 36.8% year-on-year in Jan–May 2026** (1.37 million tonnes cumulative), with May 2026 alone at 310,000 tonnes (+61% y/y) (Fastmarkets, monthly China UCO/biodiesel export tracking, 2026).

These two figures are not contradictory: they describe the same redirection this report's hypothesis assumes — U.S.-bound Chinese UCO is down sharply, while China's overall exports (increasingly Europe-bound) are up. The corollary is that Asian UCO is being reallocated rather than displaced, which is the trade dynamic Singapore's Europe/Asia-Pacific repositioning (Section 2.3) is built around.

**Why this matters for Singapore.** Singapore-based trading houses have historically helped aggregate and finance Asia-origin UCO cargoes bound for U.S. renewable diesel and SAF refiners. From 2026, that route is likely to become much less attractive for U.S.-bound volumes because non-North American feedstock will no longer qualify for 45Z. Asian UCO is therefore more likely to be redirected toward Europe, while U.S. demand shifts toward domestic, Canadian and Mexican material. Singapore will need to adapt from Asia-to-U.S. UCO intermediation toward other roles, including traceability, trade finance, offtake structuring and Latin America-linked supply corridors. Singapore trading houses' experience structuring cross-border agricultural commodity flows (soy, palm) is directly transferable to the Argentina–Canada soy oil corridor feeding Braya (Section 3, Canada), and to potential Latin America–Canada feedstock deals more broadly.

**HS-code caveat.** No customs code isolates "SAF" itself — it clears customs blended under jet-fuel codes (2710.19/2710.12 family) or isn't separately declared, since blending and book-and-claim structures mean the physical fuel and the sustainability claim often travel separately. HS-level trade data is a useful proxy for **feedstock** flows but cannot directly measure SAF trade. Relevant feedstock codes: **1518** (UCO — also captures other hydrogenated/modified fats, so not cleanly isolated), **1502** (animal fats/tallow), **1507/1511/1514** (soybean/palm/canola oil), **2207** (ethanol), **3826** (biodiesel/blends), **2710.20** (fuel oils containing biodiesel). Any trade-data-based claim in this study should be scoped to feedstock flows, not finished SAF volumes — this applies to the China UCO figures above and to the Argentina–Canada soy oil corridor.

Best free sources for verifying these flows: **USITC DataWeb** (US-side UCO/tallow imports by origin, HTS 10-digit, 1989–present), **Comex Stat/MDIC** and **ABIOVE** (Brazil), **INDEC** (Argentina, monthly "Comercio Exterior" bulletins — the primary source needed to independently confirm the Braya-feeding Argentina soy-oil corridor), and **UN Comtrade** / **WITS** for cross-country baselines and tariff-driven diversion analysis. Judgment call carried over from the source review: trade data is best used to corroborate feedstock re-routing narratives (China→Europe, Argentina→Canada), not to estimate SAF volumes directly.

### 2.4 Singapore's SAF Targets and Strategy

The Civil Aviation Authority of Singapore's (CAAS) Sustainable Air Hub Blueprint, launched in 2024, sets a national SAF uplift target of 1% for all flights departing Singapore from 2026, rising to 3–5% by 2030, subject to global SAF availability.

- **Financing model:** rather than a supplier-side blending mandate (the EU/UK model), Singapore uses a mandatory SAF Levy on passengers, cargo and general/business aviation departing Changi and Seletar, collected into a statutory SAF Fund. **Correction:** ticket sales begin 1 April 2026 as previously stated, but trade reporting indicates CAAS deferred actual flight-departure applicability of the levy from 1 October 2026 to **1 January 2027** in a March 2026 announcement. This should be verified directly against caas.gov.sg before it is cited further in the deck.
- **Central procurement:** the purpose-built Singapore Sustainable Aviation Fuel Company Ltd. (SAFCo) manages procurement, allocation and administration of SAF and its environmental attributes on behalf of the Fund, and is separately launching a voluntary SAF procurement trial to aggregate corporate demand alongside the regulated levy.
- **Cost impact:** Government estimates put the 2026 levy at roughly S$1–3 for short-haul economy tickets (e.g., Bangkok) rising to about S$6.40–16 for long-haul (e.g., London), depending on the vintage of the estimate; SAF itself is estimated to cost three to four times conventional jet fuel.
- **Regional platform:** CAAS launched the Asia-Pacific Sustainable Aviation Centre (APSAC) in July 2025 to conduct region-specific SAF policy research and explore regional feedstock options. This gives Singapore a platform to engage producers and policymakers on how Americas-origin SAF supply could connect to Asia-Pacific demand.

## 3. Market Deep Dives

This section examines the three priority Americas markets in depth: the United States, Canada and Brazil. Each profile covers the policy environment, feedstock base, production capacity and key players, R&D landscape, market outlook, and a specific Singapore opportunity angle.

### United States

**Policy environment.** U.S. SAF policy underwent its most significant change in years with the OBBBA (signed July 4, 2025), which restructured the 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit — the successor to the expired 40B Sustainable Aviation Fuel Credit and biodiesel blenders' credit. Key features:

- Extended through December 31, 2029 (from an original 2027 sunset), giving producers multi-year investment certainty.
- Maximum SAF credit value cut from $1.75/gallon to $1.00/gallon (a 43% reduction), removing the SAF-specific premium that had existed over non-aviation clean fuels; base statutory rates are around $0.35/gallon for SAF and $0.20/gallon for non-aviation fuel, scaling up to 5x with prevailing-wage-and-apprenticeship (PWA) compliance.
- Feedstock restricted to material produced or grown in the U.S., Canada or Mexico (the most consequential change for global feedstock trade).
- Indirect land-use change (ILUC) penalties removed from emissions calculations for fuel produced after Dec 31, 2025, and "foreign entity of concern" ownership restrictions added, with Treasury issuing 170 pages of proposed implementing regulations on Feb 3, 2026 (comment period closed April 2026).
- The FAA's Fueling Aviation's Sustainable Transition (FAST) grant program — which had awarded $291 million across 22 projects in August 2024 — had its unobligated balances rescinded under OBBBA, with **no further rounds confirmed** (FAA FAST Grants page). This shifts the policy burden from grants to tax credits and shifts the centre of gravity to state-level programmes — most importantly California's LCFS and Washington's Clean Fuel Standard, both of which publish live credit/deficit generation and price data (CARB LCFS Data Dashboard, monthly/quarterly; WA Dept. of Ecology CFS data, quarterly) and are the best available public proxies for actual transacted SAF/renewable-diesel volumes and pricing in the U.S. market. Tax incentives also exist in Illinois, Iowa, Colorado, Arkansas and Minnesota. The original policy ambition remains the Biden-era SAF Grand Challenge target of 3 billion gallons of domestic SAF production by 2030, though the OBBBA's reduced SAF-specific incentive value makes that target considerably harder to reach on current trajectories.

**Feedstock.** The U.S. feedstock base is dominated by used cooking oil, animal tallow, soybean oil and corn/distillers' corn oil, supplemented — pre-2026 — by substantial imported UCO (over 3 billion pounds annually by 2023, with more than half from China). The North American-content restriction in 45Z is expected to redirect demand toward domestic corn, soy and canola oil, and toward Canadian and Mexican tallow and UCO — with U.S. UCO imports overall showing trade diversion (shifting suppliers) rather than a volume collapse through 2025 (see Section 2.2 for the corroborating China trade-flow data). EIA's Monthly Biofuels Capacity & Feedstocks Update and EPA's RIN Generation Data (EMTS, by D-code) are the best free, regularly-updated sources for tracking this shift directly.

**Production capacity and key players.** U.S. SAF production capacity grew roughly 1,400% from early 2024 through 2025, though from a very low base.

- **Diamond Green Diesel** (Valero / Darling Ingredients JV) — the world's second-largest renewable diesel producer at 1.2 billion gal/yr across Port Arthur, TX and Norco, LA — completed a SAF conversion project at Port Arthur in Q4 2024 giving it nameplate SAF capacity of 235 million gal/yr (up to 50% of that plant's 470 million gal/yr renewable diesel capacity). Confirmed directly via Valero's SEC 10-K and Darling Ingredients' investor filings.
- **World Energy**, Paramount, CA — the world's first commercial-scale SAF facility (converted from an existing refinery, producing since 2016), operating at roughly 250 million gal/yr, with customers including United, JetBlue, Amazon Air and Etihad. **Project-risk update:** Air Products exited the companies' $2 billion expansion partnership in February 2025; World Energy's existing 250 million gal/yr operations are unaffected, but the expansion's financing path is now an open question.
- **Montana Renewables** (Calumet), Great Falls, MT — expanding SAF output from ~30 million gal/yr toward a targeted 150 million gal/yr in 2026 and 300 million gal/yr by 2028. **Correction to loan figure:** the DOE loan guarantee structure is $782 million in a first tranche plus $150 million in Calumet equity, under Title 17 §1706 (Energy Infrastructure Reinvestment program), which closed in January 2025; total financing including capitalized interest is $1.67 billion — more precise than the previously-cited flat "$1.44 billion" figure (DOE Loan Programs Office, energy.gov/lpo).
- **Phillips 66** (Rodeo Renewed, CA) brought roughly 10,000 barrels/day of SAF capacity online in Q3 2024, though production was temporarily halted in Q4 2024.
- **Neste** has built out U.S. distribution via its Galena Park terminal on the Houston Ship Channel (33.5 million gal of blending/storage capacity) and, via the Martinez Renewables JV with Marathon Petroleum, a California conversion project.
- Roughly 110 million gallons of neat SAF were supplied in the U.S. in the most recent full year cited by industry trackers, of which about 70 million gallons were imported — underscoring that even the U.S., with the region's largest capacity base, still runs a SAF deficit against airline demand.

**R&D landscape.** U.S. SAF R&D runs through DOE national labs (Bioenergy Technologies Office, NREL) on advanced pathways (alcohol-to-jet, Fischer-Tropsch, power-to-liquid), the DOE's 45ZCF-GREET lifecycle-emissions model that underpins 45Z eligibility determinations, and a dense web of land-grant university programmes tied to feedstock carbon-intensity science (Iowa State, Illinois, Nebraska) — most immediately relevant through USDA's Feedstock Carbon Intensity Calculator (FD-CIC), still being finalised as of April 2026, which will determine how much credit value climate-smart agricultural practices unlock under 45Z. No SAF-specific NREL/PNNL publications were surfaced in the source review beyond this general programme structure; cite DOE's Bioenergy Technologies Office / 45ZCF-GREET documentation directly rather than named lab reports unless a follow-up search finds something more specific.

**Market outlook.** The U.S. market should continue to add SAF capacity through 2026–2028 as already-financed projects come online, including Montana Renewables' expansion and further Diamond Green Diesel debottlenecking. However, the policy environment is less straightforward than headline capacity figures suggest. The 2029 sunset, lower SAF credit value, pending 45ZCF-GREET/FD-CIC finalisation, and North American feedstock requirement all create uncertainty for project economics. The main shifts to watch are: greater use of domestic, Canadian and Mexican feedstock; stronger reliance on state-level incentives such as California and Washington's programmes; and lower attractiveness of Asian feedstock for U.S.-bound supply chains.

**Singapore opportunity.**
- **Trading/structuring:** help finance and structure Canadian and Mexican feedstock flows into U.S. 45Z-eligible supply chains, since these now carry a policy-driven premium over non-North American material.
- **Certification and traceability:** Singapore-based sustainability certification and blockchain-traceability expertise (relevant given Acelen's Finboot blockchain partnership in Brazil, discussed below) is transferable to U.S. producers needing to prove North American feedstock origin under 45Z's new rules.
- **Book-and-claim intermediation:** Singapore-based corporates and financial institutions can participate in book-and-claim SAF certificate purchases (the Google/American Airlines model, transacted via the SAFc Registry — Section 1.2) as part of their own Scope 3 decarbonisation, building commercial relationships with U.S. producers without needing physical Singapore-U.S. fuel logistics.

### Canada

**Policy environment.** Canada's federal Clean Fuel Regulations (CFR), in force since 2023, require liquid fossil fuel suppliers to reduce the carbon intensity of the fuels they supply through a tradable-credit system, but the CFR does not itself mandate SAF use; SAF is simply one eligible low-carbon fuel among many that can generate credits. Credit-generating fuel mix in 2024 was dominated by ethanol (61.3%) and renewable diesel (27.9%), with SAF contributing just 0.8% — confirmed directly by ECCC's quarterly Clean Fuel Regulations credit market reports (canada.ca), evidence that the CFR alone is not yet moving material SAF volume.

British Columbia's Low Carbon Fuel Standard (BC-LCFS) became the first jurisdiction in North America to mandate SAF blending, requiring 1% SAF in jet fuel by 2028, rising to 2% (2029) and 3% (2030), alongside jet fuel carbon-intensity reduction targets of 2% (2026) rising to 10% (2030). Vancouver International Airport separately offers incentive payments of CA$0.75–1.20 per litre for low-carbon jet fuel use. **Data-source flag:** no CARB-equivalent public credit dashboard for BC-LCFS was confirmed in the source review; a live registry likely sits under gov.bc.ca's low-carbon-fuels programme but this needs direct follow-up before the study leans on BC-specific compliance data.

In response to U.S. policy shifts eroding demand for Canadian-produced biofuel, the federal government introduced a time-limited Biofuels Production Incentive (over CA$370 million over two years, plus a separate CA$1.5 billion Clean Fuels Fund) for domestic biodiesel and renewable diesel producers from January 2026, and British Columbia introduced Canadian-content requirements effective January 1, 2026 requiring the province's 5% renewable-fuel minimum to be met with Canadian-produced material.

**Feedstock.** Canada's domestic feedstock base (canola oil, tallow, used cooking oil) is insufficient for its own renewable fuel ambitions, and producers increasingly import — most notably **Braya Renewable Fuels** in Newfoundland, which sources soy oil from Argentina (757,000 tonnes in 2024, up sharply from 48,000 tonnes in 2023). This figure should be cross-checked against INDEC's monthly "Comercio Exterior" bulletins (Section 2.2) as the primary Argentine-side source. Argentina's own emerging SAF ambitions (YPF's Santa Fe Bio venture — Section 5) are worth watching as a factor that could, over time, compete with rather than purely supply the Argentina–Canada corridor; today India, not North America, still takes the majority of Argentine soy oil exports, so the corridor is a small share of Argentina's total export base and has room to grow before that tension becomes material.

**Production capacity and key players.**
- **Braya Renewable Fuels** (the former Come By Chance refinery, Newfoundland) began renewable diesel production in February 2024 with 824 million litres/year capacity, and has stated plans to add SAF production and explore green hydrogen; 100% of current output is exported to the United States, since it is not logistically feasible to serve high-consumption Canadian regions from Newfoundland.
- **Tidewater Renewables** (Prince George, BC) began commercial renewable diesel production in November 2023 (170 million litres/yr), and has publicly welcomed BC's Canadian-content rules as "levelling the playing field with imported biofuels." Tidewater is a confirmed recipient of NRCan Clean Fuels Fund support (~CA$342 million).
- **Parkland Corporation** (Burnaby, BC) produced Canada's first batch of low-carbon jet fuel via co-processing in late 2024, and is working with industry to raise the ASTM D1655 limit on approved renewable co-processing feedstock from 5% toward 30%.
- **Imperial Oil** is in advanced stages of bringing online a roughly 1-billion-litre renewable diesel facility near its Strathcona refinery in Edmonton, Alberta, backed by ~CA$720 million in NRCan Clean Fuels Fund support — one of several new capital projects (alongside Covenant Energy in Saskatchewan and Varennes Carbon Recycling in Quebec) reshaping Canadian low-carbon fuel supply. **Data-source note:** NRCan's Clean Fuels Fund has no single consolidated public project table; the Imperial Oil and Tidewater figures above come via CER market snapshots and company press releases rather than one database, so treat NRCan-funded project figures as provisional pending a more systematic sweep.

**R&D landscape.** Canadian SAF-relevant R&D centres on Natural Resources Canada's Clean Fuels Fund (de-risking hydrogen, renewable diesel, renewable natural gas, cellulosic ethanol, synthetic fuels and SAF projects), provincial low-carbon fuel credit-market design research, and applied engineering work on co-processing feedstock ratios (Parkland's ASTM pathway work).

**Market outlook.** Canada's near-term SAF outlook is bifurcated: British Columbia will generate mandated SAF demand from 2028, while the rest of the country relies on the CFR's general credit market, which has so far generated negligible SAF volume. Export orientation (Braya to the U.S.) makes Canadian producers exposed to the same 45Z North American-content dynamics affecting U.S. domestic producers — a potential tailwind if Canadian feedstock displaces disqualified Asian material, but a headwind if U.S. policy or trade friction disrupts cross-border flows.

**Singapore opportunity.**
- **BC-LCFS mandate compliance:** Singapore-based SAF traders and certifiers can support BC-obligated parties in sourcing CORSIA/ISCC-eligible SAF ahead of the 2028 mandate, particularly if Canadian domestic capacity lags the mandate timeline.
- **Cross-border feedstock structuring:** Singapore trading houses' experience structuring cross-border agricultural commodity flows (soy, palm) is directly transferable to the Argentina–Canada soy oil corridor feeding Braya, and to potential Latin America–Canada feedstock deals more broadly — the same logic set out in Section 2.2's discussion of the U.S. feedstock re-nationalisation.
- **Watching-brief on U.S. spillover:** because Braya exports 100% of output to the U.S., Canadian project economics are effectively a derivative of 45Z policy; Singapore engagement here should be paced against U.S.-side developments rather than treated as an independent Canadian opportunity.

### Brazil

**Policy environment.** Brazil's "Fuel of the Future" Law (Lei No. 14.993/2024, enacted October 9, 2024) is the most comprehensive biofuels legislative package in the Americas, and includes the National Sustainable Aviation Fuel Programme (ProBioQAV) which — unlike blend-volume mandates elsewhere — sets a well-to-wake emissions-reduction target for domestic flights: 1% by 2027, rising 1 percentage point per year to 10% by 2037.

ProBioQAV sits alongside two other pillars of the law: the National Green Diesel Programme (PNDV), which raised the mandatory biodiesel blend from B14 (2025) to B15 (2026) en route to B20 by 2030; and an ethanol-gasoline blending ceiling raised to 30% (E30), up from 27%, with Brazil's National Energy Policy Council (CNPE) empowered to set the actual annual rate within that range. These sit on top of the pre-existing RenovaBio carbon-credit programme (2019), which requires fuel distributors to retire CBIO credits against a national decarbonisation target — 48.09 Mt CO₂ in 2026, rising to 72.54 Mt CO₂e by 2034 versus a 2018 baseline. CBIO issuance, registration and retirement data, plus B3 exchange pricing, is tracked fortnightly on ANP's public "Painel Dinâmico da Plataforma CBIO" dashboard — the central live data source for this study's Brazil section.

Brazil's National Agency for Petroleum, Natural Gas and Biofuels (ANP) is expected to publish detailed SAF production and marketing regulations in the second half of 2026 — the remaining piece of regulatory clarity the market is waiting on before further final investment decisions.

**Feedstock.** Brazil's feedstock advantage is unmatched in the Americas: soybean oil at industrial scale, an established sugarcane-ethanol base suited to Alcohol-to-Jet (ATJ) conversion (an estimated 2 billion litres of ethanol would be needed to meet ProBioQAV's 1.2-billion-litre 2037 SAF demand via ATJ alone), used cooking oil, and — distinctively — macaúba, a native, non-food oilseed palm being cultivated at industrial scale specifically for SAF feedstock. **Verification flag:** the underlying claim about Embrapa/University of São Paulo macaúba and soy-residue research could not be independently confirmed in the source review — no SAF-specific publications from either institution surfaced — so this should be treated as directionally plausible but unconfirmed until a direct repository search is done. Feedstock-supply figures underneath these claims should be checked against CONAB (soybean/sugarcane crop data), Comex Stat/MDIC (customs data, HS-code level) and ABIOVE's monthly soybean-complex export reports.

**Production capacity and key players.**
- **Acelen Renewables** (Mubadala Capital) — the standout Americas SAF project. Acelen secured $1.5 billion in financing in May 2026 (part of a total investment exceeding $3 billion) for a biorefinery in São Francisco do Conde, Bahia, targeting 1 billion litres/year of combined SAF and renewable diesel via HEFA technology from 2029, using soybean oil, used cooking oil and macaúba grown across 144,000 hectares of degraded land (20% earmarked for family-farmer partnerships). Financing was arranged by a 10-institution consortium led by HSBC and the IFC, including the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank and Bank of China; roughly 90% of commercialisation volumes are already contracted, with named commercial partners including Trafigura, Moeve, Bunge and BGN, and the U.S. and Europe targeted as primary export markets. Trade press (S&P Global, ESG Today, bioenergyinternational.com) is the best current sourcing for Acelen — no dedicated English-language IR page was found.
- **Jobs figures — do not conflate.** Two different metrics circulate for this project and should be kept separate: trade press cites **~3,600 peak construction jobs**, while a Fundação Getulio Vargas (FGV) economic-impact study estimates the project's **full value chain could generate up to $40 billion and roughly 85,000 direct and indirect jobs over a ten-year horizon**. These are not the same measurement (point-in-time construction employment vs. a full-value-chain, decade-long estimate) and should not be presented interchangeably. The underlying FGV PDF itself was not located in the source review (only secondary coverage via clickpetroleoegas.com) — a direct portal.fgv.br search is needed before citing the $40bn/85,000-jobs figure as a primary-sourced number.
- **Petrobras** (state oil major) plays a central role in Brazil's broader fuel-pricing and blending system and is expected to be a significant SAF offtaker/producer as ANP's 2026 regulations clarify the domestic market. **Sourcing caveat:** Petrobras has no SAF-specific investor disclosures yet (checked via SEC EDGAR / Petrobras IR); claims about its SAF role should lean on ANP filings and trade press rather than implying IR-level confirmation.
- **Traceability infrastructure:** Acelen signed a 12-month partnership with Finboot in March 2026 to deploy a blockchain-based traceability platform ("Marco Track & Trace") covering land eligibility, farm-level production output, an emissions calculator, and tokenization of sustainability attributes — a direct response to the credibility requirements of EU and U.S. SAF buyers. Finboot's own case-study page (finboot.com/acelen-use-case) is a stronger primary source for the partnership's actual scope than the generic trade-press coverage cited in the prior draft.

**R&D landscape.** Brazilian SAF R&D is anchored by RBQAV (the Brazilian SAF industry association — rbqav.com.br, a direct primary source for this section), UNICA (sugarcane industry) research into ATJ pathway economics, and Insper's **Agro in Data** programme (agro.insper.edu.br/en/agro-in-data), which runs a dedicated, English-language HEFA-vs-ATJ comparison series specific to Brazil. The University of São Paulo and Embrapa are commonly cited as providing agronomic research relevant to macaúba cultivation and soy residue valorisation, but see the feedstock-section flag above — this specific claim is unconfirmed.

**Market outlook.** Brazil is, on the balance of the evidence gathered for this report, the clearest structural winner in the Americas SAF race over a 2027–2035 horizon: it combines (i) the region's largest and most diversified feedstock base, (ii) a legislated, if gradual, domestic demand signal from 2027, (iii) a marquee, substantially-financed flagship project (Acelen) that de-risks the investment case for follow-on capital, and (iv) explicit export orientation toward the two largest global SAF demand centres (EU, US). The principal risks are regulatory — ANP's SAF-specific rules are still pending — and execution risk on a first-of-scale integrated agro-industrial project with a 2029 start date.

**Singapore opportunity.**
- **Trade finance and structuring:** Acelen's ~90% pre-contracted volumes and 10-institution international financing consortium (HSBC, IFC, AIIB, Bank of China) demonstrate that large-scale Brazilian SAF projects already draw on Asian and Gulf capital; Singapore-based banks and trading houses have a comparable value proposition — particularly for follow-on projects that won't have Acelen's first-mover backing — and should be positioned ahead of the next wave of Bahia-region or macaúba-linked financings.
- **Certification and traceability partnerships:** the Acelen–Finboot blockchain traceability model (land eligibility, farm-level output, emissions calculator, tokenization) is a template Singapore's own sustainability-certification and fintech/traceability ecosystem could replicate or partner into for other Brazilian producers preparing for EU/US buyer due diligence, especially once ANP's 2026 rules create a second wave of financeable projects.
- **Offtake and book-and-claim linkage:** with the U.S. and Europe named as Acelen's primary export markets, Singapore-based corporates and airlines could explore book-and-claim purchases of Brazilian SAF certificates (mirroring the Google/American Airlines/SAFc Registry model — Section 1.2) as a way to secure Scope 3 credentials tied to a credible, large-scale Latin American project without requiring direct Singapore-Brazil fuel logistics.
- **APSAC as an engagement channel:** Singapore's Asia-Pacific Sustainable Aviation Centre (Section 2.4) is a natural forum for structured dialogue with Brazilian producers and ANP on how Bahia-origin SAF could connect to Asia-Pacific demand, mirroring the kind of early-stage technical relationship Germany's GIZ has built with Colombia (Section 5).

## 5. Watchlist Markets (Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Mexico)

These four markets are carried at reduced depth in this draft, consistent with the narrowed US/Brazil/Canada focus. Full profiles for Chile, Colombia and Mexico remain in `workingdraft.md` and are not repeated here in full; this section flags what's changed or newly-sourced since that draft, plus the comparative positioning already captured in Section 4. Argentina is a new addition to the watchlist this pass — it previously appeared only as a feedstock source inside the Canada profile (Section 3) — reflecting a newly-sourced, SAF-specific project (Santa Fe Bio) that gives it a stronger case than a pure feedstock-exporter framing implies.

**Argentina.** Argentina has no SAF-specific blending mandate, but its general biofuels programme (ethanol at 12%, biodiesel at 7.5%) was extended by the lower house through 2030, and the Energy Secretariat has opened consultations on raising the biodiesel blend to B15 to absorb domestic soybean-oil surplus (S&P Global Commodity Insights, Dec 2025). Argentina is the world's largest soybean oil exporter, but the majority of shipments currently go to India rather than North America — a caveat against reading too much into the Argentina–Canada soy-oil corridor (Section 3, Canada) as a large share of Argentina's total export base. Export taxes (retenciones) on soy oil/meal are on a legislated step-down path — 24.5% as of March 2025, cutting monthly through 2027 toward 15% by end-2028 (USDA FAS GAIN reporting) — which should improve domestic crush-sector economics and could pull some soybean oil toward domestic biodiesel/SAF use rather than export. The clearest project-level development is **Santa Fe Bio**, a ~$400 million joint venture announced in August 2025 between state oil major YPF and Essential Energy to build a SAF/HVO facility at YPF's San Lorenzo refinery, planned in two phases and financed partly through Argentina's Large Investment Incentive Regime, using used cooking oil and crop residues as feedstock and explicitly positioned for export given San Lorenzo's port access (TradingView/Reuters, Hydrocarbon Processing, SAF Investor, Aug 2025). **Verification flag:** disclosed capacity, phase timing and the YPF/Essential Energy ownership split were not found in public reporting during this pass — treat Santa Fe Bio as a confirmed but not yet sized project until a primary YPF or Essential Energy source is located. For Singapore, Argentina is currently more of a watching-brief than an active opportunity: the near-term angle is the same cross-border feedstock structuring already identified for the Argentina–Canada corridor (Section 3, Canada), with Santa Fe Bio worth tracking as a potential second angle — either as a future direct engagement, or as a signal that Argentina's own SAF ambitions could eventually compete with, rather than only supply, North American demand.

**Chile.** Chile's relevance is entirely through Haru Oni (HIF Global, Punta Arenas) as the world's first integrated e-fuels demonstrator (FID 2021), producing e-gasoline/e-methanol rather than e-SAF today. HIF's capital has since migrated toward other geographies, including a planned $4 billion e-fuels plant in Brazil (announced February 2026) — worth cross-referencing against the Brazil deep-dive (Section 3) given the geographic overlap. No SAF-specific blending mandate exists in Chile.

**Colombia.** Aerocivil's SAF Roadmap (Resolution 00090, January 2025) targets 100 million gallons of domestic SAF by 2035 and 450 million gallons by 2050. Ecopetrol has run small test batches at Cartagena and, with GIZ, is studying a green hydrogen-based e-SAF pilot. **New, better-sourced figure:** MIT's "Toward Sustainable Decarbonization of Aviation in Latin America" study (news.mit.edu/2025; sustainability.mit.edu) — the actual primary source behind the prior draft's secondary paraphrase — finds Colombia could reach **~250 million litres/year of SAF potential with a 20% increase in palm/sugarcane output**, and that regional SAF costs **$1.11–2.86/litre versus ~$0.70/litre for conventional jet fuel** across the six Latin American countries the study examined. This should be cited directly going forward rather than through secondary coverage. As with Petrobras, **Ecopetrol has no SAF-specific investor disclosures** — lean on Aerocivil/ANP-equivalent filings and trade press.

**Mexico.** No binding SAF mandate or biofuels law as of mid-2026; Pemex retains effective control over domestic fuel production. Mexico's UCO base is a net export commodity today, feeding the same U.S./Europe flows discussed in Section 2.2, rather than a domestic SAF input. Remains a policy-trigger watch item — a Sheinbaum-era biofuels law would materially re-rate the opportunity — rather than a market justifying dedicated Singapore capital or partnership commitment today.
